## **Chapter 1: The Kepler Space Telescope – A Galactic Census of Exoplanets**

### **1.1 Instrument Profile**

* **Instrument Name:** Kepler Space Telescope.<sup>1</sup>  
* **Instrument Type:** Kepler was a dedicated space telescope, but more specifically, it was a highly sensitive photometer designed to detect the minuscule dips in starlight caused by transiting exoplanets.<sup>2</sup> Its sole scientific instrument was built around a 1.4-meter primary mirror and a powerful digital camera, which at the time of its launch was the largest ever flown in space. This camera consisted of 42 charge-coupled devices (CCDs) that combined to create a 95-megapixel detector, allowing it to simultaneously monitor the brightness of over 150,000 stars.<sup>4</sup>  
* **Year Became Operational:** The spacecraft was launched from Cape Canaveral on March 7, 2009, and officially entered service, beginning its science mission, on May 12, 2009\.<sup>1</sup> The primary mission, which involved continuously staring at a single field of stars, was brought to an end in May 2013 after the failure of a second of its four reaction wheels, which were necessary for precise pointing.<sup>7</sup> However, engineers developed a clever new mode of operation that used solar pressure to help stabilize the spacecraft, enabling a new mission called K2. The K2 mission became fully operational in June 2014 and continued to discover exoplanets and study other astrophysical phenomena until the spacecraft was retired.<sup>2</sup> Kepler's nine-year mission officially concluded on October 30, 2018, when it finally ran out of the fuel needed for science operations.<sup>8</sup>  
* **Operating Agency/Organization:** The Kepler mission was operated by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA).<sup>8</sup> It was a collaborative effort involving several NASA centers and industry partners. NASA's Ames Research Center was responsible for the ground system, mission operations, and scientific data analysis, while the Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) managed the mission's development.<sup>1</sup> The flight system itself was built by Ball Aerospace & Technologies Corporation, a key partner throughout the mission's long development.<sup>9</sup>

### **1.2 Key Scientific Contributions**

#### **1.2.1 From Scarcity to Ubiquity: Establishing that Planets Outnumber Stars**

Kepler's most profound and enduring legacy is the fundamental re-calibration of humanity's understanding of its place in the cosmos, achieved by answering the question of planetary prevalence. Before Kepler's launch in 2009, fewer than 400 exoplanets had been discovered, and their overall abundance in the galaxy was a matter of speculation.<sup>10</sup> The mission was specifically designed to move beyond individual discoveries and provide a statistical answer to the question, "How common are other worlds?".<sup>10</sup>

To achieve this, Kepler employed a novel strategy: for four years, it stared unblinkingly at a single, star-rich patch of the sky in the constellation Cygnus, continuously monitoring the brightness of more than 150,000 stars.<sup>2</sup> This approach generated the first large, unbiased statistical sample of planets, allowing astronomers to extrapolate their findings to the galaxy as a whole. The result of this galactic census was revolutionary. By the end of its mission, Kepler had identified thousands of planet candidates, with 2,662 of them confirmed as exoplanets—more than half of all exoplanets known at the time.<sup>1</sup> The statistical analysis of this unprecedented dataset led to a startling conclusion: on average, there is at least one planet for every star in the Milky Way.<sup>2</sup> This means that our galaxy is home to billions of hidden planets—more planets than stars.<sup>2</sup> This discovery transformed planets from cosmic rarities into standard components of stellar systems, fundamentally altering the context for the search for life.

#### **1.2.2 The Dominance of Small Worlds: Uncovering the "Kepler Demographics"**

Beyond simply counting planets, Kepler's high-precision photometry allowed it to determine their sizes with remarkable accuracy. This capability led to a second major discovery: the most common type of planet in the galaxy is a size for which there is no analog in our own solar system. These worlds, often categorized as "Super-Earths" or "mini-Neptunes," have radii between that of Earth and Neptune (which is about four times Earth's radius).<sup>11</sup>

Prior to Kepler, detection methods like the radial velocity technique were heavily biased toward finding massive, "hot Jupiter"-type planets orbiting very close to their stars. Kepler's ability to detect the tiny dimming caused by much smaller planets opened the floodgates to this previously hidden population.<sup>12</sup> The data revealed that small planets far outnumber large ones, a demographic pattern that provides powerful observational support for the core accretion model of planet formation, in which planets grow from the accumulation of solid materials before they accrete large gas envelopes.<sup>14</sup> The discovery of Kepler-37b, a planet smaller than Mercury and only slightly larger than our Moon, demonstrated that the population of small planets extends down to sub-Earth sizes, suggesting that such tiny worlds are common.<sup>15</sup>

This finding has profound implications for astrobiology and the search for habitable worlds. The sheer abundance of small, potentially rocky planets dramatically increases the number of potential targets for follow-up studies. Further analysis of the Kepler dataset concluded that between 20% and 50% of Sun-like stars are likely to host a small, possibly rocky planet within their habitable zone—the orbital region where surface temperatures could permit liquid water.<sup>11</sup> Kepler was the first mission to find such worlds, including Kepler-22b, the first planet discovered in the habitable zone of a star similar to our Sun, and Kepler-186f, the first Earth-sized planet found in a habitable zone.<sup>3</sup>

#### **1.2.3 Revealing the Diverse Architectures of Planetary Systems**

The third major contribution of the Kepler mission was to reveal the astonishing diversity of planetary system architectures, demonstrating that the configuration of our own solar system is far from a universal template. The mission's rich dataset provided a comprehensive look at how other solar systems are arranged, challenging and refining theories of planet formation and migration.<sup>5</sup>

One of the most surprising discoveries was the prevalence of compact, multi-planet systems. Kepler found numerous systems where multiple planets orbit their host star at distances much smaller than Mercury's orbit around the Sun.<sup>11</sup> The Kepler-11 system, for example, contains six planets all orbiting closer to their star than Venus does to ours.<sup>2</sup> In 2017, the Kepler-90 system was confirmed to host eight planets, making it the first and only other system known to have as many planets as our own solar system, though its planets are packed into a much smaller volume.<sup>5</sup> The existence of these tightly packed systems raises critical questions about how they form—whether the planets are "born" in place or form farther out and migrate inward over time.<sup>11</sup>

Kepler also expanded the known zoology of planetary systems by discovering worlds in exotic configurations. It provided the first definitive proof of circumbinary planets—worlds orbiting two stars, akin to the fictional planet Tatooine from *Star Wars*. The discovery of Kepler-16b, a Saturn-sized planet orbiting a binary star system, demonstrated that stable planetary orbits are possible in such complex gravitational environments.<sup>1</sup> Furthermore, Kepler found evidence that planet formation is a process that began very early in the history of the universe. The discovery of the Kepler-444 system, which contains five Earth-sized planets, was particularly notable because its host star is 11.2 billion years old. This means these planets formed when the universe was less than 20% of its current age, proving that terrestrial-sized planets have been forming for most of cosmic history.<sup>18</sup>

## **Chapter 2: The Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) – Peering into the Cold Universe**

### **2.1 Instrument Profile**

* **Instrument Name:** Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA).<sup>21</sup>  
* **Instrument Type:** ALMA is a radio telescope array, a powerful interferometer designed to function as a single, reconfigurable telescope.<sup>21</sup> It consists of 66 high-precision antennas—54 with 12-meter diameters and 12 with 7-meter diameters—that can be arranged in various configurations with baselines extending up to 16 km.<sup>21</sup> This array is located on the Chajnantor Plateau in the Chilean Andes at an extreme altitude of over 5,000 meters (16,400 feet), where the dry, thin atmosphere minimizes the absorption of the faint millimeter and submillimeter waves from space.<sup>23</sup> By combining the signals from all antennas, ALMA can achieve an angular resolution superior to that of the Hubble Space Telescope at visible wavelengths.<sup>25</sup>  
* **Year Became Operational:** The path to ALMA was a long one, with construction beginning in the early 2000s.<sup>26</sup> Early scientific observations using a partial array of 16 antennas commenced in September 2011, even while construction was ongoing.<sup>21</sup> The observatory was officially inaugurated and declared fully operational in March 2013\.<sup>22</sup>  
* **Operating Agency/Organization:** ALMA is a global scientific partnership, representing one of the most ambitious and expensive ground-based astronomical projects in history.<sup>22</sup> It is a collaboration between the European Southern Observatory (ESO), the U.S. National Science Foundation (NSF), and the National Institutes of Natural Sciences (NINS) of Japan. These primary partners represent consortia of numerous national institutions from Europe, North America (including Canada), and East Asia (including Taiwan and South Korea). The observatory is operated in cooperation with the Republic of Chile, the host nation.<sup>22</sup>

### **2.2 Key Scientific Contributions**

#### **2.2.1 Imaging Planetary Genesis: The Revolutionary View of Protoplanetary Disks**

One of ALMA's most iconic and scientifically transformative achievements came in 2014 with its observation of the young star HL Tauri.<sup>31</sup> The resulting image was the sharpest ever taken at submillimeter wavelengths and provided a revolutionary view into the process of planet formation. For the first time, astronomers could see the intricate structure of a protoplanetary disk—the rotating disk of dense gas and dust surrounding a newborn star from which planets are formed.

The image of HL Tauri revealed a stunning series of concentric, bright rings separated by dark gaps.<sup>31</sup> This was the first clear and unambiguous visual evidence that planets were actively forming within the disk. The gaps are regions where nascent protoplanets are sweeping up dust and gas, carving out their orbits and accumulating mass.<sup>32</sup> This single observation fundamentally altered the scientific understanding of planetary formation timelines. The star HL Tauri is estimated to be only about one million years old, yet its disk already showed the well-defined structures indicative of advanced planet formation. This suggested that the process of building planets happens much more rapidly than most prior theories had predicted.<sup>32</sup> Since this initial breakthrough, ALMA has conducted large-scale surveys of hundreds of protoplanetary disks, such as the AGE-PRO program, to systematically study how their gas and dust content evolves over millions of years, providing critical data to constrain and refine models of planetary system architecture.<sup>34</sup>

#### **2.2.2 Tracing Cosmic Origins: The Detection of Oxygen in the Early Universe**

Leveraging its extreme sensitivity to the faint spectral lines emitted by atoms and molecules, ALMA has become the world's premier instrument for chemical analysis of the early universe. It has repeatedly broken the record for detecting the most distant chemical elements, providing crucial insights into when the first generations of stars began to enrich the cosmos with elements heavier than hydrogen and helium.

In 2018, ALMA detected the spectral signature of ionized oxygen in the galaxy MACS1149-JD1. The light from this galaxy traveled for 13.28 billion years to reach Earth, meaning astronomers were observing it as it existed just 500 million years after the Big Bang.<sup>32</sup> This discovery pushed back the frontier of the chemically mature universe. More recently, in early 2025, ALMA achieved an even more remarkable feat by detecting oxygen in JADES-GS-z14-0, the most distant galaxy confirmed to date.<sup>30</sup> This galaxy is seen as it was less than 300 million years after the Big Bang, when the universe was only 2% of its current age.<sup>30</sup>

The implications of these detections are profound. Oxygen is not created in the Big Bang; it is forged in the nuclear furnaces of massive stars and released into the interstellar medium when those stars die. The presence of significant quantities of oxygen so early in cosmic history indicates that at least one, and likely multiple, generations of massive stars must have already formed, lived out their short lives, and exploded as supernovae. This suggests that the processes of star formation and galaxy assembly were far more rapid and efficient in the primordial universe than many leading cosmological models had previously assumed.<sup>30</sup>

#### **2.2.3 A Critical Role in Imaging a Black Hole's Shadow**

ALMA was an indispensable component of the Event Horizon Telescope (EHT), the global scientific collaboration that in 2019 captured the first-ever direct image of a black hole's shadow.<sup>31</sup> The EHT operates on the principle of Very Long Baseline Interferometry (VLBI), linking radio telescopes across the globe to create a virtual, Earth-sized observatory capable of achieving the extraordinary angular resolution needed to image the event horizon of a supermassive black hole.<sup>39</sup>

Within this global array, ALMA served as the anchor station. Its large collecting area (from its 66 antennas) and high sensitivity at millimeter wavelengths increased the overall sensitivity of the EHT by an order of magnitude.<sup>40</sup> This enhancement was critical; it transformed the EHT from an experiment that could merely detect a signal into a true imaging array capable of resolving the structure around the black hole.<sup>40</sup> ALMA's central location in the array and its advanced calibration capabilities were essential for producing the final, high-fidelity images.<sup>32</sup>

The EHT collaboration first successfully imaged the 6.5-billion-solar-mass black hole at the center of the galaxy Messier 87 (M87).<sup>31</sup> In 2022, the collaboration released the first image of Sagittarius A\*, the four-million-solar-mass black hole at the center of our own Milky Way galaxy.<sup>42</sup> These images provide stunning visual confirmation of predictions made by Einstein's General Theory of Relativity in the most extreme gravitational environment known to exist. Subsequent analysis of ALMA's polarimetry data was also crucial for mapping the powerful magnetic fields at the edge of the M87 black hole, providing clues as to how it launches its colossal relativistic jet.<sup>41</sup>

## **Chapter 3: The Gaia Space Observatory – Charting the Milky Way's DNA**

### **3.1 Instrument Profile**

* **Instrument Name:** Gaia Space Observatory.<sup>46</sup>  
* **Instrument Type:** Gaia is a dedicated astrometry space telescope, designed to conduct the most precise and comprehensive census of the Milky Way ever undertaken.<sup>47</sup> Its instrumentation consists of two telescopes pointing in different directions, which continuously scan the sky as the spacecraft rotates.<sup>49</sup> This design allows it to measure the positions, parallaxes (which yield distances), and proper motions (movements across the sky) of stars with extraordinary accuracy.<sup>50</sup> In addition to its astrometric instruments, Gaia is also equipped with photometric and spectroscopic instruments to measure the brightness, color, temperature, and chemical composition of the stars it observes.<sup>51</sup>  
* **Year Became Operational:** The Gaia spacecraft was launched on December 19, 2013\.<sup>46</sup> After a period of commissioning and travel, it arrived at its operational orbit around the second Sun-Earth Lagrange point (L2) and officially began its science mission on July 25, 2014\.<sup>52</sup> After more than a decade of groundbreaking observations, its science operations concluded on January 15, 2025, as its supply of cold gas for attitude control was depleted.<sup>46</sup>  
* **Operating Agency/Organization:** Gaia is a mission of the European Space Agency (ESA).<sup>47</sup> The mission operations are conducted from ESA's European Space Operations Centre (ESOC) in Germany, while the science operations and data processing are managed by the European Space Astronomy Centre (ESAC) in Spain, in collaboration with the pan-European Gaia Data Processing and Analysis Consortium (DPAC).<sup>48</sup>

### **3.2 Key Scientific Contributions**

#### **3.2.1 The Definitive Map: A 3D Stereoscopic and Kinematic Census of the Galaxy**

Gaia's primary and most monumental achievement is the creation of the largest, most precise three-dimensional map of the Milky Way galaxy ever assembled. The mission has cataloged the positions, distances, and motions of nearly two billion celestial objects, primarily stars, which represents about 1% of the total stellar population of the galaxy.<sup>49</sup>

The precision of Gaia's measurements represents a quantum leap in astrometry. For the brightest stars, its positional accuracy is up to 200 times greater than that of its predecessor, the Hipparcos mission, and its catalog is 20,000 times larger.<sup>49</sup> This level of precision is equivalent to measuring the diameter of a human hair from a distance of 1,000 km.<sup>49</sup> This definitive dataset has allowed astronomers to fundamentally revise the structural model of our galaxy. Gaia's data confirmed that the Milky Way is a barred spiral galaxy, but it overturned the long-held idea of four prominent spiral arms. Instead, the map reveals a more complex structure with multiple, less-prominent minor arms.<sup>59</sup> It also showed that the galaxy's central bar is more elongated and inclined with respect to the Sun's position than previously thought, and that the diffuse stellar halo surrounding the galaxy is not spherical but is stretched and tilted like a football.<sup>58</sup>

#### **3.2.2 Galactic Archaeology: Uncovering the Milky Way's Violent Merger History**

By measuring the precise 6D phase-space information (3D position and 3D velocity) for an enormous number of stars, Gaia has enabled the field of "galactic archaeology." This discipline uses the motions and chemical compositions of stars as fossils to reconstruct the history of the Milky Way's formation. Gaia has revealed that our galaxy's past was far from tranquil, showing clear evidence of numerous mergers with smaller dwarf galaxies that have been torn apart and consumed over cosmic time.<sup>53</sup>

A landmark discovery in this field was the identification of the "Gaia-Sausage-Enceladus" (GSE) merger. Gaia's data revealed a large population of stars moving on highly elongated, radial orbits in the opposite direction to the majority of the galaxy's stars.<sup>58</sup> These stars are the remnants of a significant dwarf galaxy that collided head-on with the nascent Milky Way approximately 10 billion years ago. This single, massive merger event is now understood to have profoundly sculpted our galaxy, contributing a significant fraction of the stars in the inner halo and "puffing up" the early galactic disk to form what is now known as the thick disk.<sup>53</sup>

Building on this discovery, Gaia's data has allowed astronomers to construct a "family tree" of the Milky Way, identifying at least six distinct stellar streams and groups that originated from different ancient mergers.<sup>53</sup> These include the recently identified Shakti and Shiva streams, which appear to be among the earliest building blocks of the galaxy.<sup>63</sup> Gaia has also provided definitive evidence for the ongoing collision with the Sagittarius Dwarf Galaxy, showing that its repeated passages through our galaxy's disk are responsible for the large-scale warp and ripples observed in the stellar distribution.<sup>53</sup>

#### **3.2.3 Revealing Unseen Structures: The Discovery of the Radcliffe Wave**

In 2020, a team of astronomers using Gaia's 3D map of interstellar dust and gas announced the discovery of the largest known coherent gaseous structure in the Milky Way: the Radcliffe Wave.<sup>64</sup> This colossal structure is a long, thin, undulating filament of interconnected stellar nurseries, stretching for 9,000 light-years through our local spiral arm.<sup>66</sup>

The discovery of the Radcliffe Wave fundamentally revised the map of our local galactic neighborhood. For over a century, astronomers had studied a collection of nearby star-forming regions known as the "Gould Belt," which was thought to be an expanding ring of stars and gas centered near the Sun. The precise distance measurements from Gaia revealed that the Gould Belt was not a ring at all, but rather a two-dimensional projection on the sky of the much larger, three-dimensional Radcliffe Wave.<sup>65</sup> Our own Sun is located just 500 light-years from the wave at its closest point and is thought to have passed through it about 13 million years ago.<sup>67</sup>

The existence of such a massive, coherent structure was entirely unexpected. More recent analysis of Gaia's data on the motion of young star clusters embedded within the wave has shown that the structure is not static. Instead, it is actively oscillating, moving up and down through the galactic plane like a traveling wave.<sup>68</sup> The origin of this wave is still a subject of research, but it may have been triggered by a past interaction, such as the passage of a dwarf galaxy through the Milky Way's disk.<sup>66</sup>

## **Chapter 4: Advanced LIGO – The Dawn of Gravitational-Wave Astronomy**

### **4.1 Instrument Profile**

* **Instrument Name:** Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory (LIGO). The discoveries detailed in this report were made possible by the significantly upgraded "Advanced LIGO" (aLIGO) detectors.<sup>69</sup>  
* **Instrument Type:** LIGO is a gravitational wave detector.<sup>71</sup> It is not a telescope in the traditional sense, as it does not detect electromagnetic radiation. Instead, it consists of two identical, L-shaped interferometers located 3,000 km apart in Hanford, Washington, and Livingston, Louisiana.<sup>71</sup> At each facility, a powerful laser beam is split and sent down two 4-km-long arms, which are housed in ultra-high vacuum tubes. The beams reflect off mirrors at the ends of the arms and are recombined. A passing gravitational wave minutely stretches and squeezes spacetime, causing an infinitesimal change in the relative lengths of the two arms. This change is detected as a shift in the interference pattern of the recombined laser light.<sup>73</sup>  
* **Year Became Operational:** The initial LIGO detectors operated from 2002 to 2010 but did not achieve a detection.<sup>69</sup> Following a major overhaul, the far more sensitive  
  **Advanced LIGO** detectors began their first official observing run in **September 2015**.<sup>70</sup>  
* **Operating Agency/Organization:** LIGO is a major scientific facility funded by the U.S. National Science Foundation (NSF). It was designed, built, and is operated by a collaboration between the California Institute of Technology (Caltech) and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT).<sup>70</sup> The scientific discoveries are made and published by the international LIGO Scientific Collaboration (LSC), which includes hundreds of scientists worldwide, in close partnership with the Virgo Collaboration in Europe and, more recently, the KAGRA Collaboration in Japan.<sup>76</sup>

### **4.2 Key Scientific Contributions**

#### **4.2.1 Confirmation of a Century-Old Prediction: The First Direct Detection of Gravitational Waves**

On September 14, 2015, mere days after the Advanced LIGO detectors were switched on for their first engineering run, both instruments recorded a clear, unambiguous signal: the characteristic "chirp" of a passing gravitational wave.<sup>78</sup> This event, designated GW150914, was the first-ever direct detection of gravitational waves and a landmark achievement in the history of science.<sup>74</sup> It provided the definitive confirmation of a key prediction made by Albert Einstein's General Theory of Relativity in 1916, a full century before the detection.<sup>74</sup>

The discovery was revolutionary, as it opened an entirely new, non-electromagnetic window on the universe. For the first time, humanity could "hear" the vibrations of spacetime itself, allowing astronomers to study the "warped side" of the cosmos—phenomena that are invisible to traditional light-based telescopes.<sup>83</sup> Analysis of the GW150914 signal revealed its cataclysmic origin: the final, violent merger of a pair of stellar-mass black holes. The black holes, with masses approximately 29 and 36 times that of the Sun, had spiraled into each other 1.3 billion years ago.
