Carpobrotus edulis and Ailanthus altissima represent classic examples of plant species that have traversed continents, originating from distant regions in Africa and Asia before finding new homes in Europe, including Italy. These introductions, often driven by human activities such as horticulture, landscaping, and erosion control, highlight the complex interplay between globalization, ecology, and biodiversity. While both species offer certain benefits—such as ornamental value or rapid growth—they also pose challenges as potentially invasive plants, altering local ecosystems and competing with native flora. This detailed overview draws on botanical databases, scientific literature, and historical records to explore their origins, distributions, introduction histories, ecological impacts, and management considerations, providing a comprehensive perspective on their journeys and implications.

Beginning with *Carpobrotus edulis*, commonly known as the Hottentot fig or ice plant, this succulent ground-creeping perennial belongs to the Aizoaceae family and the genus *Carpobrotus*. Native to the Cape Provinces of South Africa, it thrives in arid, sandy coastal environments where its fleshy leaves store water, enabling survival in harsh conditions. The species features vibrant yellow or pink flowers and edible fig-like fruits, which have historically been used by indigenous communities for food and medicinal purposes. Its natural distribution is confined to southern Africa, but human intervention has dramatically expanded its range. Introduced to Europe in the early 1900s primarily as an ornamental plant and for stabilizing dunes, *C. edulis* quickly naturalized in Mediterranean regions. In Italy, it is particularly prevalent along the Tyrrhenian coast, where it forms dense mats on beaches, cliffs, and disturbed sandy sites, often outcompeting native vegetation. Records indicate its presence in Sicily and other coastal areas, where it has been documented as invasive, reducing biodiversity in habitats like dunes and rocky shores. Ecologically, *C. edulis* alters soil chemistry by increasing salinity and nutrient levels, which can inhibit the growth of other plants, and its rapid vegetative propagation via rooting stems facilitates swift colonization. Studies on its chilling tolerance suggest adaptations that have aided its northward expansion in Europe, including Italy. Management efforts in Italy involve manual removal and herbicide applications, though its resilience makes eradication challenging. Interestingly, recent research explores its potential in skincare and as a bioindicator, leveraging its antioxidant properties derived from extracts. For detailed distribution maps and taxonomic data, the Plants of the World Online (POWO) entry confirms its native status in Africa and introduced presence in Italy, among other global locations such as Australia, California, and the Iberian Peninsula. The genus *Carpobrotus*, encompassing about 20 species mostly from South Africa, is characterized by its succulent habit and daisy-like flowers, with some species hybridized in cultivation.

Shifting focus to *Ailanthus altissima*, known as the tree of heaven, this deciduous tree from the Simaroubaceae family and genus *Ailanthus* originates from central and eastern China, including regions like North-Central China, Southeast China, and Taiwan. It is adapted to temperate climates, growing rapidly up to 25 meters tall with compound leaves and clusters of small, greenish flowers that produce winged seeds for wind dispersal. Historically valued in China for silk production (via the Ailanthus moth) and traditional medicine, its introduction to Europe dates back to the mid-18th century. In Italy, it was first planted in 1760 at the Botanical Garden of Padua, initially for ornamental and experimental purposes related to silk farming. From there, it spread across the country, naturalizing in all regions and becoming one of the most widespread invasive trees. Its prolific seed production, allelopathic chemicals that suppress other plants, and tolerance to pollution and poor soils have enabled it to thrive in urban areas, roadsides, and disturbed woodlands throughout Italy, from Sicily to the Alps. In Rome and the Latium region, for instance, it colonizes archaeological sites, causing structural damage through root penetration. Ecologically, *A. altissima* shifts herbaceous layer richness, favoring nitrophilous species and reducing overall plant diversity in invaded forests. Biological control options, such as the fungus Verticillium dahliae, have shown promise in Italy, causing wilt and decline in affected trees. The POWO database lists its extensive introduced range, including Italy, confirming its Asian origins and global invasiveness in over 50 countries. The genus *Ailanthus* includes a few species native to Asia and Australia, noted for their fast growth and resilience, though *A. altissima* is the most widely distributed.

In broader context, both species exemplify the Columbian Exchange's extended effects, where plants from the Old World and New World (though here from Africa and Asia) were redistributed globally post-1500s, with introductions accelerating in the 18th-20th centuries. In Italy, alien flora inventories show a significant portion originating from Asia (about 20%) and Africa (15%), with *C. edulis* and *A. altissima* among the notable invaders. National strategies address these through monitoring and removal, as outlined in biodiversity reports. Counterarguments to their "invasive" label include potential benefits, like *C. edulis* for erosion control or *A. altissima* for urban greening, but evidence leans toward net ecological harm in non-native ranges. Future research may focus on climate adaptability and biocontrol, ensuring balanced management that respects biodiversity while acknowledging human-mediated dispersal.
