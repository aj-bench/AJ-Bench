Crouzon syndrome, first described in 1912 by French physician Octave Crouzon, is a rare autosomal dominant genetic disorder primarily resulting from mutations in the fibroblast growth factor receptor 2 (FGFR2) gene. This mutation disrupts normal bone growth, leading to premature fusion of certain cranial sutures (craniosynostosis), which affects the shape of the head and face. Unlike related conditions such as Apert syndrome, Crouzon syndrome does not typically involve syndactyly or other abnormalities of the hands and feet, aligning precisely with the described features. Prevalence estimates suggest it occurs in approximately 1 in 25,000 to 1 in 60,000 live births, making it one of the more common syndromic forms of craniosynostosis.

The pathophysiology centers on gain-of-function mutations in FGFR2, which accelerate bone formation and suture closure. Common cranial involvement includes the coronal sutures, leading to brachycephaly (short, wide head), turribrachycephaly (tower-like skull), or even cloverleaf skull in severe cases. Facial manifestations often include midface hypoplasia (underdeveloped upper jaw and cheekbones), hypertelorism (wide-set eyes), proptosis (bulging eyes), and a beaked nose. These features can vary in severity, and while intelligence is usually normal, complications may impact development if unmanaged.

If left untreated, Crouzon syndrome can result in significant morbidity. Elevated intracranial pressure from restricted brain growth may cause headaches, developmental delays, or hydrocephalus. Ocular issues, such as exposure keratitis or conjunctivitis due to proptosis, can lead to vision loss if the eyes are not protected. Auditory complications, including conductive hearing loss from middle ear anomalies or Eustachian tube dysfunction, affect up to 50% of cases. Respiratory challenges, like obstructive sleep apnea from narrowed airways, pose risks, particularly in infancy. Rarely, untreated cases may progress to intellectual disability or blindness, though timely intervention mitigates these risks.

Treatment is multidisciplinary and tailored to symptom severity, often involving a team of craniofacial surgeons, neurosurgeons, otolaryngologists, ophthalmologists, orthodontists, and genetic counselors. Surgical interventions are cornerstone, typically beginning in infancy. Cranial vault remodeling or fronto-orbital advancement corrects skull shape and relieves pressure, often performed between 6-12 months of age. Midface advancement procedures, such as Le Fort III osteotomy, address facial hypoplasia later in childhood (around 5-10 years). Distraction osteogenesis gradually expands bones for better results with less relapse. Non-surgical options include helmet therapy post-surgery for skull molding, hearing aids, tracheostomy for severe airway issues, and speech/language therapy. Genetic counseling is essential for family planning, as there's a 50% inheritance risk per pregnancy.

Long-term prognosis is generally good with early treatment, with most individuals achieving normal life expectancy and function. However, multiple surgeries may be needed, and psychosocial support is crucial due to facial differences. Ongoing monitoring for complications like chiari malformation or syringomyelia is recommended.
