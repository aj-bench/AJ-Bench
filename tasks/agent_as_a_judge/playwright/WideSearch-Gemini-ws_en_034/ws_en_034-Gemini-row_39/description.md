## **Market Analysis: England**

The housing market in England, by virtue of its scale and high average property values, exerts a significant influence on the overall UK trend. As of June 2025, the average house price in England stood at £290,956.<sup>3</sup> The market's trajectory over the 2022-2025 period demonstrates a pronounced sensitivity to changes in borrowing costs. The higher absolute price point in England compared to the other UK nations means that identical increases in mortgage interest rates translate into larger absolute increases in monthly repayments for homeowners and prospective buyers. This fundamental arithmetic has been the primary driver of the market's behaviour, leading to a sharp correction from the peak values seen in 2022 and, more recently, to the emergence of profound internal divergences.

## **Market Analysis: Wales**

The property market in Wales has charted its own distinct course through the turbulent 2022-2025 period. As of June 2025, the average house price in Wales was £209,728, with an annual growth rate of 2.6%.<sup>3</sup> This rate of growth is more subdued than that seen in Scotland or the stronger English regions, but it signifies a market that has weathered the period of high interest rates without experiencing the deep corrections seen in parts of southern England.<sup>4</sup>

The performance of the Welsh market can be understood in comparison to its larger neighbour, England. At times, Wales has outperformed the English average, particularly during the market slowdown, as its lower absolute price point provided a degree of insulation from the most severe affordability pressures. However, its growth has recently lagged behind the resurgent northern English regions and Scotland. This performance is influenced by a unique set of factors, including a different composition of housing stock, the significant economic influence of cross-border buyers from more expensive regions of England, and the impact of devolved policies. Wales operates its own property tax system, the Land Transaction Tax (LTT), which has different thresholds and rates compared to England's Stamp Duty Land Tax (SDLT), creating different incentives for buyers and movers.

A more granular view of the market's structure is possible by examining the available data on buyer and funding status. In June 2025, the average price paid by a first-time buyer in Wales was £180,000. This contrasts with the average price of £251,000 paid by a former owner-occupier.<sup>2</sup> This significant gap highlights the challenge for those seeking to enter the market and underscores the financial advantage held by existing homeowners who have accumulated equity. The data also shows a relatively even split in the market between cash and mortgage-funded purchases, with both categories showing similar average prices around £210,000 in June 2025, though annual growth was slightly higher for mortgage-funded properties (2.8%) than for cash purchases (2.2%).<sup>2</sup> This suggests a balanced market with participation from a range of buyer types, from first-time buyers to downsizers and investors.

## **Market Analysis: Scotland**

The Scottish housing market has demonstrated remarkable resilience and strength throughout the review period, frequently outperforming the other nations of Great Britain. In the 12 months to June 2025, house prices in Scotland increased by 5.9%, the highest annual growth rate among the three GB nations. This brought the average property price to £191,927.3 This robust performance was consistent, with data from earlier in the period, such as the 5.7% annual increase recorded in February 2025, confirming a trend of sustained growth even as other parts of the UK faltered.<sup>6</sup>

The primary factor underpinning this market resilience is affordability. Scotland's average house price is substantially lower than that of England and, more recently, has been lower than that of Wales. This lower absolute price point has acted as a protective buffer, shielding the market from the most severe impacts of the affordability squeeze caused by rising interest rates. Much like the North East of England, the lower entry cost meant that mortgage payments remained more manageable for a larger proportion of the population, sustaining effective demand and supporting price levels.

Scotland's distinct legal framework for property transactions, which typically involves an "offers over" bidding system, also contributes to different market dynamics and price discovery compared to England and Wales. While Registers of Scotland publishes its own set of non-mix-adjusted statistics, the analysis in this report relies on the UK HPI data to ensure a methodologically consistent, like-for-like comparison with the other nations.<sup>12</sup> The UK HPI data confirms that Scotland's property cycle appears to be at a different stage of maturity compared to the high-priced regions of southern England, suggesting there was more room for growth before hitting the acute affordability ceilings that constrained those markets.

The national average for Scotland, while strong, conceals significant local and regional variations. The detailed data available at the local authority level reveals a diverse internal market.<sup>6</sup> For instance, in February 2025, areas like East Renfrewshire and Fife saw double-digit annual price growth (10.6% and 10.4% respectively), while other areas like Aberdeenshire experienced a slight decrease (-2.4%). Major urban centres such as Edinburgh and Glasgow also displayed their own unique trends. This internal diversity highlights the importance of looking beyond national averages to understand the complex interplay of local economic factors that drive property markets.

## **Market Analysis: Northern Ireland**

The housing market in Northern Ireland operates with a distinct set of economic fundamentals and has followed a unique trajectory. Based on the official quarterly data, the average house price in Quarter 2 (April to June) 2025 was £185,108. This represented a strong year-on-year price growth of 5.5%, placing Northern Ireland among the best-performing regions of the United Kingdom during that period.<sup>3</sup>

As previously noted, the analysis of Northern Ireland's market must account for its quarterly data cycle. Therefore, the focus of interpretation is on quarter-on-quarter and year-on-year trends, which provide a more reliable picture of market movements than any attempt to infer monthly changes. The consistent and strong year-on-year growth figures throughout the 2022-2025 period indicate a market with sustained upward momentum.

This performance can be contextualized within a longer-term narrative of recovery and "catch-up" growth. The Northern Ireland property market experienced one of the most severe corrections in the UK following the 2008 global financial crisis, and prices have been on a gradual recovery path since. The recent period of strong growth can be seen as a continuation of this trend, supported by the fact that Northern Ireland has the lowest absolute average house price of the four UK nations. This superior affordability has been a crucial advantage during a time of rising interest rates.

Furthermore, the market is influenced by a unique economic geography. Its deep ties to the Great Britain economy are complemented by its land border with the Republic of Ireland, which provides a connection to the EU single market. This creates a distinct set of economic drivers and influences not present elsewhere in the UK. The market structure is also different, being less dominated by a single, high-value metropolitan hub like London. Alternative data sources, such as the Ulster University House Price Index, provide additional qualitative context on these local market dynamics.<sup>13</sup> The strong growth observed in recent years suggests that the Northern Ireland housing market retains significant potential relative to other parts of the UK, marking it as a notable outlier.
