# **Synthesis and Key Findings**

## **Summary of Key Statistical Findings**

The verified data provides a clear statistical portrait of the girls' single-sex high school sector in Seoul. Based on the comprehensive verification process, this report has confirmed detailed information for 32 such institutions. Of these, the vast majority are privately operated, with 28 private schools to only 4 public schools. This stark 7-to-1 ratio underscores the historical and ongoing dominance of the private sector in providing single-sex education for girls in the capital.

The average student enrollment also differs between the two sectors. The four public schools have an average enrollment of 626 students, reflecting their mandate to serve defined local populations. The 28 private schools show a slightly higher average enrollment of 675 students, though this figure encompasses a much wider range, from smaller, specialized schools to large, city-wide autonomous institutions. The distribution of founding years confirms the two major waves of establishment: a foundational period from the 1880s to the 1930s for the historic private schools, and a major expansionary period for both public and private schools from the 1960s through the 1980s.

## **Identification of Outliers and Noteworthy Trends**

The dataset reveals several noteworthy outliers that highlight the diversity and stratification within this educational sector.

* **Oldest Institution:** **Ewha Girls' High School**, founded in 1886, is the oldest girls' high school in Seoul and a true pioneer of female education in Korea.<sup>16</sup>  
* **Most Recent Institution:** Among the verified schools, **Kyungbok Girls' High School**, founded in 1993, is the most recently established, representing the tail end of the 20th-century expansion period.<sup>28</sup>  
* **Largest Enrollment:** **Ewha Girls' High School** has the largest student body with 1,203 students, a testament to its enduring prestige and large campus.<sup>16</sup> It is closely followed by the autonomous  
  **Sehwa Girls' High School** with 1,105 students, demonstrating the significant drawing power of schools with special admissions privileges.<sup>24</sup>  
* **Smallest Enrollment:** **Shingwang Girls' High School**, with 266 students, has the smallest enrollment among the verified schools, suggesting a more intimate or specialized educational environment.<sup>27</sup>

The data confirms the trend of stratification within the private sector. A clear hierarchy exists, with historic schools and autonomous schools at the top, often correlated with larger student bodies and city-wide recruitment, followed by a broad middle tier of well-regarded district-level private schools, and a lower tier of smaller institutions.

## **Concluding Remarks: The Enduring Legacy and Future Trajectory**

The landscape of girls' single-sex high schools in Seoul is a product of over 130 years of history, reflecting the nation's journey through modernization, conflict, rapid development, and intense educational competition. The data presented in this report confirms the continued vitality and relevance of these institutions, particularly in the private sector, where a rich legacy and a reputation for academic excellence have created powerful and enduring brands.

While the broader national trend in recent decades has moved towards co-education, Seoul remains a bastion of single-sex schooling. The concentration of historic, prestigious, and high-performing girls' schools in the capital ensures their place in the top tier of the educational hierarchy. These institutions are not merely schools; they are powerful social and cultural entities with deep roots and influential alumni networks that continue to shape Korean society. The data provides a robust, verified baseline for understanding this unique and important sector. Future analysis can build upon this directory to explore academic outcomes, demographic trends in enrollment, and the ongoing evolution of single-sex education in one of the world's most competitive academic environments.
